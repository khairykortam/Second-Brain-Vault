Analysis sections may answer at least one of these four questions:

1. How does concept X work in the prototype? (example: motor)
2. How did concept X improve the results?
3. How did I obtain result X? What calculations and assumptions did I use?
4. What additional insights can I extract       from the results? (example: power coefficient)

Example Scenario for further understanding:
If you get a new idea for the analysis, check whether it clearly answers one or more of the four questions. Only pursue ideas that do so, unless you believe you have a solid idea that falls outside them. In that case, do not rely on yourself, team members, or ChatGPT to check if its viable. Always ask your capstone assistant to review it.

Get quick external feedback before you write. Ask a capstone assistant, a team member, or ChatGPT. Give them your idea, a short description of the capstone, and the four questions above. If asking ChatGPT, only treat a firm yes as approval; yes-no-ish respones are not enough. If you do not get external feedback before you write, you'll risk wasting an entire day or more writing a sub section of the analysis which could get deleted. You don't want that.

Capstone assistants are busy, so if you're going to ask them about ALL your analysis ideas, only go to them once you have a list of four to five ideas with brief explanations showing how each idea demonstrates understanding of the capstone and the scientific basis (as explained in Hazem’s guide).

Keep in mind these questions are only suggestions. They are simply a fast way to filter out most weak or irrelevant ideas. Many good analysis ideas will naturally answer one or more of the four questions (the more the better). However, there can still be strong ideas that do not fit neatly into these categories. If you think you have such an idea, it is crucial to check with your capstone assistant to confirm that it is viable.
